Two Kings and a Stolen Throne

The Pahlavi Era

One night in March 1976, Iranians went to sleep in the year 1355 and woke up in the year 2535.

Nothing had happened to the sun or the calendar of the universe. The government had simply decided to stop counting the years from the journey of the Prophet Muhammad, the way Muslims everywhere count them, and start counting instead from the day a king named Cyrus founded an empire two and a half thousand years earlier. Overnight, by order of the shah (Persian for king, شاه), the country jumped twelve centuries into an imaginary past.

That single strange decision tells you almost everything about the Pahlavi era. For fifty-four years, two kings tried to build a brand-new, modern Iran by reaching back over a thousand years of Islam to grab the glory of the ancient Persians. They built railways and universities, schools and clinics and an army; they also jailed their critics and, in the end, lost everything. This is the dynasty most living Iranian families still argue about, because their own grandparents lived it. So we have to be careful: the goal is not to decide who was hero and who was villain, but to see what actually happened, and to notice where even now we honestly do not know.

Two men, one family, fifty-four years

The Pahlavi dynasty had exactly two kings. The father, Reza Shah, ruled from 1925 to 1941. The son, Mohammad Reza Shah, ruled from 1941 to 1979. In the middle of the son's reign sits a single year, 1953, that Iranians still cannot stop talking about. Hold those three things in your head: the father, the son, and the year in the middle. They are one connected story.

The family name itself is a clue. "Pahlavi" was not an old royal surname; it was the name of an ancient pre-Islamic Persian language. When Reza Khan picked it for his new dynasty, he was making a loud claim about where he thought Iran's real roots lay: not in the Islamic centuries, but far behind them, with the old kings.

The soldier who crowned himself

Start with the father, because his beginning is the least likely part of the whole story. Reza Khan was born around 1878 in a poor mountain village in the north of Iran. His father died when he was a baby. He had no royal blood, no rich family, almost no schooling. He joined the army and rose through a Russian-trained force called the Cossack Brigade. In February 1921, with the country weak, broke, and half-occupied after the First World War, he marched his soldiers into the capital and took over the government. He made himself minister of war, then prime minister in 1923, and in 1925 he pushed aside the last shah of the old Qajar dynasty and put the crown on his own head. A boy from a mountain village with no name and no money ended up founding a dynasty. How?

Here is the first place to be careful, and the first fork in the road. For a long time people told a simple story: the British put Reza Khan in power as their puppet. British officers were certainly nearby in 1921, and Britain was glad to see a strong man take charge. But historians who have studied it closely (Homa Katouzian and Cyrus Ghani among them) argue the puppet story is too neat. Reza Khan turned out to be his own man, often against British wishes, and the people around him did not know he would end up a king. They thought they were getting one more general in a country that had seen many. The crown was not waiting for him. He took it.

Once he had it, he built. Here you have to hold two true things at once, because squashing them into one tidy verdict is how you lie about him.

True story number one: Reza Shah dragged Iran toward the modern world, fast. He built a national army that could actually control the country. He built the Trans-Iranian Railway, courts, modern schools, and the University of Tehran. He set up a single national government where before there had been tribes, local strongmen, and chaos.

True story number two: he was a dictator. He crushed the tribes and the power of the clergy by force. He took land for himself. He silenced, jailed, and sometimes killed people who criticized him. And in January 1936 he did something that still divides Iranian families: in a policy called kashf-e hijab (کشف حجاب, "the unveiling"), he banned the Islamic veil. Not as a suggestion. Police tore the chador and headscarf off women in the street. For some women this was the liberation they wanted. For many devout women it was a violation so total that they refused to leave their own homes for years. Both of those women existed. Both were Iranian. Both lived under the same king.

So which is the real Reza Shah, the builder or the bully? "He modernized Iran" and "he ran a dictatorship" are not two stories you have to choose between. They are one story about one man: a country can be hauled forward and held down by the same hand at the same time.

It was Reza Shah's government, in 1935, that asked the world to stop saying "Persia" and start saying "Iran." Iranians had called the country Iran among themselves for ages; "Persia" was mostly an outsiders' word. The change was aimed at foreigners, and it was tangled up with the era's fashion for "Aryan" pride: a word ancient Iranians used for themselves, and that twentieth-century race-theory then poisoned. Handle it with tongs.

And then comes the ending nobody would have written for him. In 1941, in the middle of the Second World War, British and Soviet armies invaded Iran and forced Reza Shah, the strongman who preached Iranian independence, off his own throne. They put his young son in his place and shipped the old king into exile; he died in 1944 in Johannesburg, South Africa, thousands of miles from the country he had ruled. What does that tell you about Iran's independence, that the king who built an army so no one could push Iran around was himself pushed off his throne by an outside army?

The year in the middle: 1953

Now the son, Mohammad Reza Shah, twenty-one years old and shaky on a throne he had not earned. To understand what he became, you have to walk through the single most argued-about year in modern Iranian history. For most of the early years the young shah was weak, and an elected parliament held real power.

In 1951 that parliament, backed by a wave of national pride, made a prime minister named Mohammad Mossadegh the most popular man in the country by doing something daring: they nationalized Iran's oil. For decades a British company had pumped Iranian oil and kept most of the money. Mossadegh said: it is our oil, in our ground, and we are taking it back.

Britain was furious. It organized a worldwide boycott and, with the United States, planned to remove Mossadegh in an operation code-named Ajax. It nearly failed. The first attempt, around August 15 and 16, 1953, fell apart; Mossadegh's people arrested the plotters, and the frightened shah fled to Baghdad, then Rome, convinced his reign was over. Then, on August 19, the plot suddenly worked. Crowds filled the streets of Tehran, the army turned, Mossadegh was overthrown, a general named Zahedi was made prime minister, and the shah flew home a "saved" king.

That much is solid. Now the genuinely hard part, where careful historians still disagree and you should be suspicious of anyone who sounds too sure.

How much of 1953 was the work of foreigners, and how much was Iranian?

This is not a settled question dressed up as a debate. It is a real, live argument among serious people:

  • Some historians, like Ervand Abrahamian, put the weight on oil and on the foreign hand: this was, above all, the CIA and British intelligence overthrowing a government that dared to take its own oil back.
  • Others, like Mark Gasiorowski, agree the foreign role was decisive but stress the Cold War fear of communism as the motive, more than oil.
  • And revisionists like Ray Takeyh and Darioush Bayandor argue the foreigners have been given too much credit, that Mossadegh had already lost crucial Iranian support, and that domestic players (the army, the clergy, the merchants of the bazaar) were what really brought him down.

Other scholars (Gasiorowski and Roham Alvandi) have hit back hard at the revisionists, calling their reading of the evidence "deeply flawed and highly selective." So the argument is alive right now, between living historians, not closed.

And here is a real "we don't know" that you can actually check is unknown: part of the record is still locked up or gone. The CIA only admitted its role officially in 2013, and the United States released its full document collection in 2017. But Britain's spy agency, MI6, has still never released its files, more than seventy years later, and some early internal accounts were destroyed. So when someone tells you exactly who was decisive in 1953, ask how they can know, with a chunk of the evidence still sealed in a British archive. We are arguing about a puzzle with pieces missing on purpose.

One more thing to be careful about, because it is easy to grab a modern measuring stick and swing it without looking at the stick. People often call 1953 "the day the West murdered Iranian democracy." There is real truth in the anger behind that, but the word "democracy" is doing heavy lifting: Mossadegh himself had, by 1953, dissolved parliament through a rigged referendum and was ruling by emergency powers. That does not excuse a foreign-backed coup. It does mean the clean picture, pure democracy on one side and foreign villains on the other, is a story we tell, not a fact we found. The honest version is messier: a popular, stubborn, partly democratic leader, undermined by foreign spies and by Iranian rivals, in a country where democracy itself was young and unfinished.

The son who remade a country

The coup changed the son. Over the next twenty-five years the frightened young king grew into something close to an absolute monarch, and the country around him changed faster than it ever had. Oil money came out of the ground in rivers, the Cold War made him a prized friend of the United States, and he had a single burning vision: to turn Iran, within one lifetime, into a modern nation the world would have to take seriously.

A great deal of what he built is still standing, and still matters. In 1963 he launched the White Revolution, a sweeping set of reforms put to a national vote. It broke up the huge old estates and handed land to millions of peasants who had never owned the ground they worked. It gave women the right to vote. And it sent the young out into the country: the Literacy Corps (sepah-e danesh, "the army of knowledge") sent high-school and university graduates, serving their military duty as teachers, into more than thirty thousand villages to bring reading and the first schools those places had ever seen. A Health Corps followed, with doctors and clinics for people who had never once seen a physician.

The building did not stop at reform. Roads, dams, and electricity reached towns that had lived by lamplight, and new universities opened their doors. Alongside the shah, his wife, Empress Farah, led a flowering of culture that reached into ordinary life. Her Kanoon (the Institute for the Intellectual Development of Children, founded in 1965) spread a web of children's libraries across the country, permanent ones in the cities and traveling ones that came to small towns by van, and its little film studio launched the directors who would make Iranian cinema famous the world over, Abbas Kiarostami among them. The yearly Shiraz Arts Festival brought music, theatre, and dance from across the world to Iran, beside Iran's own traditional performers. The Tehran Museum of Contemporary Art, opened in 1977, gathered one of the finest collections of modern art anywhere outside Europe and America, with Picasso and van Gogh and Pollock hanging in Tehran. Iran even became a quiet world leader in protecting nature: by the mid-1970s it had built one of the largest networks of national parks and wildlife refuges on earth, and in 1971 the first global treaty to protect wetlands was signed on Iranian soil, at the Caspian town of Ramsar. The royal family's charity, the Pahlavi Foundation, paid for scholarships, hospitals, and student grants at home and abroad. For a young Iranian in these years, the country was visibly getting richer, healthier, better schooled, and more connected to the world, one year to the next.

That is one of two true stories, and the second is just as real. The new Iran had almost no room for disagreement. The shah ruled alone: elections were managed, political parties were hollow, and his secret police, SAVAK (set up in 1957 with American and Israeli help), watched, jailed, and tortured those who opposed him. The oil wealth that paid for museums and universities dazzled at the top while a great many Iranians, in poor villages and on crowded city edges, saw little of it. The Pahlavi Foundation did real good, yet it also held the royal family's fortune and drew steady accusations of corruption. And some of the grandest gestures landed badly: to many devout, ordinary Iranians, an avant-garde festival full of foreign performers, or a king who counted the years from Cyrus, looked like a court performing for outsiders and turning its back on its own people. You cannot understand him by keeping only the half you like. He remade a country, and he would not let it speak.

That contradiction reached its peak in October 1971, when Mohammad Reza Shah threw one of the most expensive parties in history at the ruins of Persepolis, the old Achaemenid capital, to celebrate "2,500 years of monarchy." He raised an air-conditioned tent city in the desert, flew in food from a famous Paris restaurant, and hosted kings and presidents from around the world, while ordinary Iranians were not invited and many were poor.

The heart of it was a moment at the tomb of Cyrus the Great, where the shah stood and spoke to a man who had been dead for two and a half thousand years. Because this piece turns on that speech, here is the real thing, in his own words, not a myth about it:

"O Cyrus, great King, King of Kings... I, the Shahanshah of Iran, offer thee salutations from myself and from our nation... Cyrus, we gather today around the tomb in which you eternally rest to tell you: Rest in Peace, for we are well awake and we will always be alert in order to preserve your proud legacy."

It is genuinely moving, and it is also a sales pitch. "Shahanshah" means "king of kings" (شاهنشاه), the ancient Persian royal title; "we are awake" is the shah promising to guard Cyrus's flame. The claim underneath it is "2,500 years of unbroken monarchy," one continuous line of kings from Cyrus straight down to himself.

That claim is not true, and the truth is more interesting than the slogan. Iran's history is not one unbroken royal line. It is full of breaks, conquests, and fresh starts: Alexander smashed the first Persian empire; Arab armies ended the last pre-Islamic one; the Mongols tore through; dynasty after dynasty rose and fell, many of them not Persian at all. And the sharpest irony: the man giving the speech came from a dynasty that was forty-six years old. His own father had been a villager with no crown. He was not the heir of a 2,500-year line; he was borrowing ancient glory to decorate a very new throne.

He dreamed out loud of a "Great Civilization" (tamadon-e bozorg), a modern superpower on the Persian Gulf. And then, with astonishing speed, it all came apart. By early 1979 he had lost his throne and his country and was wandering the world looking for a place that would take him in, dying in exile in Egypt the next year. How does a ruler with an army, oil, and superpower friends lose so completely, so fast? That is its own enormous question, and its own piece. Notice only this: almost nobody saw it coming, including him.

What we genuinely do not know

The biggest open question we have already met: how much of the 1953 coup was foreign and how much was Iranian. That one is not going away until, maybe, Britain opens its files.

But there are others worth standing in front of honestly:

  • What would have happened if Mossadegh had survived? A stable democracy, a collapse, a drift toward the Soviet side? We can argue, but we cannot know. The road not taken left no records.
  • Did the forced reforms, like the unveiling, actually win hearts, or just bury resentment that exploded later? Historians can measure laws and schools; they cannot easily measure what millions of people quietly felt.
  • Why did almost no expert predict the 1979 revolution? Even with all the documents, we explain the collapse far better looking backward than anyone managed looking forward. That should make us humble about the next surprise, too.

These are not gaps you should feel sad about. They are the edge of what can be known, and standing at that edge is the most honest place to be.

The forks in the road

It is tempting to read the Pahlavi era as a machine that could only end one way: father builds, son struggles, revolution arrives. Resist that. In 1921 Reza Khan might have stayed one more forgotten general; in 1941 his throne might have held; in August 1953 the coup failed at first, with the shah a refugee in Rome who thought he would never come home. End those few days differently, and there might have been no second Pahlavi reign, no 1971 party, perhaps no 1979 as we know it. The story only looks like a straight staircase because we stand at the top, looking down.

Why it still echoes

For thousands of years, "Iran" had been an idea: a language, a way of being, a memory of old kings, carried through empire after empire even when the rulers were Arab or Turkic or Mongol and spoke no Persian at home. The Pahlavis took that old, pulsing idea and forged it into something new: a modern nation-state, with a flag, an army, a single language in every school, and a story about itself reaching all the way back to Cyrus. That is where the most ancient thread in Iran's history and the most modern argument about it finally meet, and it was the source of both their grandeur and their distortion. To build the modern nation they reached past Islam, the faith of almost everyone in the country, to grab the pre-Islamic kings; they counted the years from Cyrus and threw a party for a dead emperor. A great many Iranians, especially the religious majority, came to feel that their own kings looked past who they actually were, and when the revolution came in 1979, that feeling was part of the fuel.

So the next time you hear that Iran is an unbroken 2,500-year monarchy, you will know two things at once. You will know it is a myth, stitched together by a modern dynasty that was itself only decades old. And you will know that the real story underneath, the one of a country conquered and remade and reborn again and again, yet somehow still Iran through all of it, is far stranger and far more astonishing than the tidy slogan the shah carved over Cyrus's tomb.
