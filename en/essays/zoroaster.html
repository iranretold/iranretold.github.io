Zoroaster

The Prophet Who Slips Through Our Fingers

There is a man whose songs may be three thousand years old, who is sometimes called the first prophet in history, and whose name, when you translate it, seems to mean something close to "the man with old camels."

That is not a joke. Scholars of the oldest Iranian languages agree on almost nothing about him, except this: the last part of his name, uštra, is the ordinary word for "camel." The first part probably means "old" or "aging." So the founder of one of the world's oldest living faiths is named, roughly, Old-Camel-Guy. (Persians call him Zardosht; the Greeks reshaped Zaraθuštra into Zōroástrēs, which gives us "Zoroaster.") Start there, because it tells the truth about the whole story: we are reaching so far back, into a world that left almost no hard evidence, that most of what we think we know turns to mist the moment we grab it.

What are we even looking at?

Zoroaster was a priest and poet who lived somewhere on the Iranian plateau, or the steppes east and north of it, long before Iran had cities, coins, or written records. He preached devotion to one great god, Ahura Mazda, "the Wise Lord" (later Persians shortened it to Ormazd). And he composed songs. Those songs, the Gathas, are the heart of everything: seventeen of them, still chanted in Zoroastrian worship today, in a language so old that the priests who sing them must be taught what the words mean.

The Gathas are the closest we can ever get to his voice. They are written in a tongue scholars call Old Avestan, so close to the oldest Indian scripture, the Rigveda, that the two read like cousins raised in one house. That likeness tells us the songs are genuinely ancient, older than the Persian empire itself. Most historians think one real person composed them, and that he is the historical Zoroaster. From the songs we glimpse his world: herders, cattle, raiders at the edge of things, and one burning question about which side a person will choose, truth or the lie.

How do we know any of this?

Slow down here, because how we know is the whole game.

Zoroaster wrote nothing. Writing barely existed in his world. The Gathas were held in memory and chanted aloud, priest to priest, for perhaps a thousand years before anyone set them in ink. They were written down only under the Sasanian kings (roughly 224 to 651 CE), who had a special alphabet invented just to capture the sacred sounds. The oldest copy that still physically exists was written in 1288 CE.

Sit with that. If Zoroaster lived around 1000 BCE, the oldest book of his words we can hold is more than two thousand years younger than the man. It is like rebuilding a Bronze Age song from a copy made in the Middle Ages.

So to hear what Zoroaster actually sounded like, you go straight to the Gathas. Here is a real passage, in a careful modern translation. The prophet performs no miracles; he stands under the sky and asks his god questions:

This I ask Thee, speak to me truly, O Lord! ... Who fixed the path(s) of the sun and the stars? Who is it through whom the moon waxes, now wanes? ... Who supports both the earth below and the heavens from falling down? Who the waters and plants? ... Who, O Mazda, is the creator of Good Mind?

(Gathas, Yasna 44)

That is the genuine article: a human being, looking up, asking who made all this.

Now set it beside the legend. More than a thousand years later, Zoroastrian priests wrote books (the Denkard, the Zadspram) that gave him a hero's life. In them he laughs instead of crying at birth, and all creation rejoices; demons try to kill the baby, flinging him under stampeding cattle and into a fire, and each time something saves him. Wonderful stories, written roughly 1,800 years after he lived. They are legend, not memory. Half the craft of history is keeping the questioning poet and the laughing miracle-baby in separate boxes.

What we don't know (this is the good part)

Here is where it gets genuinely strange, and genuinely exciting.

We do not know when he lived. Not within a century. Not within five centuries. The honest range that serious scholars defend runs from about 1500 BCE down to about 600 BCE, a gap of nine hundred years. Why so wide? Two methods point in opposite directions. The old Zoroastrian tradition said he lived "258 years before Alexander," which lands near 600 BCE, just before Cyrus the Great. But that figure comes from a religious calendar that chopped history into tidy thousand-year blocks, so historians do not trust it as a real date. The other method studies the language: Old Avestan sits so close to the Rigveda that many scholars push Zoroaster back toward 1000 BCE or earlier, and Mary Boyce argued for 1200 BCE or before. The reference work experts actually reach for calls the dating "an embarrassment of long standing." A German scholar, Hermann Lommel, summed it up almost a century ago: "When and where Zarathushtra lived, one does not know." It is still the most honest sentence written about him.

We do not know where he lived, either. The geography inside the holy texts points to eastern Iran or Central Asia, not the western Iran of the later empires; later tradition tried to plant him in the west, but most scholars now move him back east. "Somewhere in the northeast, probably" is as sharp as the picture gets.

And the hardest part, which even many admirers have never heard: some serious scholars doubt the historical man wrote the Gathas at all. The poems sometimes name "Zarathushtra" in the third person, and once even speak to him: "O Zoroaster, who is your truthful ally?" If you are writing your own hymn, why address yourself by name, like a stranger? Scholars such as Jean Kellens and Prods Oktor Skjærvø argue that a real and important man named Zarathushtra lives inside these songs, but perhaps not as their author. Others, like Helmut Humbach, answer that ancient poets really did speak of themselves that way. The argument is alive right now. So we are unsure of his dates, unsure of his home, and unsure which words are truly his. We are left holding a voice without a face.

Be careful with the famous claim

You will often hear a thrilling story: that Zoroaster was the world's first monotheist, and that his religion secretly invented heaven and hell, the devil, the last judgment, and a final savior, then handed the whole package to Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. Be careful with it.

Take the "first monotheist" half. In the Gathas, Ahura Mazda is plainly the supreme god. But he is not alone. Around him stand bright beings: Truth, Good Mind, Right-Mindedness (called his daughter), and others. There is also a hostile spirit who chooses evil. Is that one god? A god with a divine family? A universe split between good and evil? The standard encyclopedia of Iranian studies says flatly that it is "pointless" to pin one label on Zoroaster's beliefs, because "he was an inspired prophet, not a systematic theologian." Calling him "the first monotheist" forces a neat modern category onto a poet who never asked our question.

Now the "he shaped the other religions" half. Zoroastrians, Jews, and Christians did live side by side for centuries under the Persian empires, and ideas surely traveled. But proving who borrowed what, and when, is brutally hard. Remember the transmission problem: the detailed Zoroastrian teachings about resurrection and the end of the world are written down clearly only in those 800s-CE books, more than a thousand years after Jewish writers were supposedly borrowing them. Albert de Jong, who has spent his career on these contacts between religions, is one of many who warn that the "evidence" for the borrowing is mostly circumstantial: suggestive, but unable to survive hard questioning.

And look hard at the measuring stick itself. "Zoroaster was first, so everyone copied him" treats being first as the prize, and treats whole religions as a relay race of stolen ideas. People reach for that story for their own reasons: to make Zoroastrianism sound grand, to make other faiths sound unoriginal, or to defend them against exactly that charge. The honest answer is less flashy: these religions grew up in the same crowded neighborhood and shaped one another in tangled ways we cannot fully map. "We are not sure, and the influence ran in messy directions" is the grown-up answer.

It almost never reached us at all

It would have been easy for every word of this to vanish. The Gathas tell us their prophet was rejected; one song opens: "What land to flee to? Where should I go to flee? From my family, from my clan they banish me." A preacher driven out by his own people, hunting for anyone who will listen. Tradition says one local ruler, Kavi Vishtaspa, finally took him in. Without that single patron, the songs might have died with the man.

They nearly died later, too. By the time the holy books were gathered, most of the Avesta was already lost; only a fraction was written down, and only a fraction of that reached us. Without the Sasanian priests' copies, and without a stubborn community to guard them through the centuries after Iran became Muslim (the Zoroastrians of Iran, and the Parsis who sailed to India), there would be no Gathas left to argue about.

And yet the thread held. Atash, fire, is still tended in Zoroastrian temples, never as a god but as a sign of purity and truth. Nowruz, the Persian New Year that Zoroastrians helped shape, is still welcomed every spring by tens of millions of Iranians of every faith. Iran's epic, the Shahnameh, still remembers a King Goshtasp, very likely the patron the Gathas point to.

That is the better trade. The myth offers a tidy "first prophet" who invented everything. The truth offers something stranger and more human: a real voice singing real questions across three thousand years of silence, and a small, stubborn community that would not let the song end.
