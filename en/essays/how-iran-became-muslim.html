How Iran Became a Muslim Country

The question that starts an argument

Try this at an Iranian dinner table: ask how Iran became Muslim. You will get answers, fast, and they will not agree.

One person will say Iran was conquered, that Arab armies smashed a glorious Persian civilization and forced a foreign religion on it at the point of a sword. Another will say no, Islam liberated Iran from a corrupt and exhausted empire, and Iranians embraced it. A third will get quiet, because for them this is not ancient history at all. It is about who they are right now.

Here is the honest thing almost nobody says first: large parts of all three stories are wrong, and the real history is more surprising than any of them. So let us slow down and look at what actually happened, separating the parts we know well from the parts we genuinely argue about. Because two different things happened, on two different clocks, and the whole confusion comes from squashing them into one.

Two clocks, not one

Clock number one: how fast did the Sasanian state, the Persian empire, fall? Answer: fast. Within about fifteen years.

Clock number two: how fast did the people of Iran become Muslim? Answer: slow. It took roughly three to four hundred years.

Keep those two clocks apart and most of the confusion dissolves. "The Arabs conquered Persia in the 600s and it became Muslim" jams them together, and the jamming is exactly where the myth lives. A government can be defeated in a single afternoon on a battlefield. What people believe in their hearts does not change on a battlefield, and it does not change in fifteen years. It changes over the lives of grandparents, parents, and children, again and again, for centuries.

Before the Arabs came: a land of many gods

Start before the conquest, because almost everyone gets the starting point wrong too.

People often imagine that pre-Islamic Iran was simply, uniformly Zoroastrian, one ancient Persian faith, pure and whole, until Islam interrupted it. That is too tidy.

Yes, Zoroastrianism was the official religion of the Sasanian Empire. It centered on the worship of Ahura Mazda, the wise lord, and on the great struggle between truth and the lie, light and dark. Fire was its sacred symbol, tended in fire temples (the Persian word atash, fire, still means exactly that in an Iranian home today). The priests, the magi, were powerful, and they worked closely with the king. The Sasanian crown and the Zoroastrian altar held each other up.

But the empire was never religiously one thing. Underneath and alongside the official faith lived:

  • Christians, and a lot of them. The Church of the East (sometimes called Nestorian) had bishops, schools, and whole communities across Mesopotamia and Iran. By the late Sasanian period this was a major, organized religion inside the empire, not a tiny fringe.
  • Jews, who had lived in Babylonia (today's Iraq, then part of the Persian world) since the deportations centuries earlier. This is the very community that produced the Babylonian Talmud, one of the central texts of Judaism. That tells you how deep and how intellectually alive Iran's Jewish world was.
  • Manichaeans, followers of the prophet Mani, who had grown up in third-century Sasanian Iran and built a new religion blending elements of several others. The state eventually persecuted them hard, which itself tells you the empire policed belief.
  • Buddhists in the east, toward Afghanistan and Central Asia, plus an assortment of local cults and older traditions that never fit neatly into any official box.

So here is the first myth to retire gently: the land Islam arrived in was not a single faith waiting to be replaced. It was a crowded, arguing, multi-religious place, where the official religion was tied to a government, and where plenty of people already lived outside that official faith.

That matters for what came next. A society used to several religions living side by side responds differently to a new one than a society of one belief would.

Why did the Sasanian Empire collapse?

Now the first clock: the fall of the state. Why did a superpower that had stood for over four hundred years come apart so quickly?

It is tempting to tell this as destiny, that the empire was old and rotten and bound to fall, that the Arab armies were meant to win. Resist that. People at the time had no idea the empire was ending. They knew battles were being lost, which is not at all the same as knowing your whole world is about to vanish. Empires lose battles and recover all the time. This time it did not recover, and we should ask why this time, not pretend the ending was written in advance.

Several real pressures stacked up, and it was the stacking that did the damage:

A ruinous war with the wrong enemy. From 602 to 628 CE, the Sasanians fought an enormous, all-out war against their old rival, the Byzantine (Eastern Roman) Empire. At first Persia won spectacularly, reaching Egypt and the walls of Constantinople. Then it lost, catastrophically, when the Byzantine emperor Heraclius struck back into the Persian heartland. Both empires came out of that war exhausted, drained of money and men. They had spent twenty-six years bleeding each other white, and then, almost immediately, a new force appeared from Arabia that neither had taken seriously.

A government that ate itself. After the Persian king Khosrow II was overthrown and killed in 628, the throne became a revolving door. In the space of just a few years, a string of rulers came and went, including, briefly, two queens. The empire's leadership was busy fighting itself over the crown at the exact moment it most needed to be united. When the Arab armies arrived, there was no steady hand on top.

Other strains historians point to, with less certainty: outbreaks of plague that may have thinned populations and treasuries, heavy taxes that pressed on ordinary farmers, and tensions between the central crown and the great noble families. How much weight each of these carried is something scholars still weigh differently. We should not pretend we can rank them precisely.

Then came the battles, and the empire fell between roughly 636 and 651 CE: about fifteen years. That part really does look like a conquest, and we should not flinch from the word. Armies came, battles were fought, an empire was destroyed, and a foreign military elite took power. Calling it anything else would be dishonest.

But how did they win those battles? That is the question almost everyone trips over, and it deserves a real answer.

How could the Arabs possibly win? The technology myth

This is the part that genuinely puzzles people, and it is a smart thing to be puzzled by. The Sasanian Empire was a superpower. It had heavy armored cavalry, war elephants, fortified cities, a professional officer class, and centuries of experience fighting Rome. The Arab armies were, on paper, lightly equipped tribesmen from the desert. So how did the tribesmen win?

Start by questioning the word hiding inside the puzzle: technology. We picture ancient battles the way we picture modern ones, where the side with jets beats the side with rifles. But in the 600s there was no gear gap like that. Both sides fought with swords, spears, bows, shields, armor, and horses. If anything, the Persians had the fancier, costlier kit: the cataphract, a rider and horse both sheathed in armor, and the war elephant. The Arabs did not beat an advanced enemy with a secret weapon. There were no secret weapons. Pre-modern wars were decided by other things: organization, mobility, leadership, morale, supply, and above all the political condition of the enemy.

And here is the single biggest clue, the fact that should reset the whole question: Persia was not the only superpower that lost. At the very same time, the same Arab armies tore Syria, Palestine, and Egypt away from the Byzantine Empire, the other great power of the age, shattering a Byzantine army at the Battle of Yarmuk in 636, the same year as Qadisiyya. Two of the most advanced empires on Earth, old enemies, both lost their richest provinces to the same newcomers within a single decade. If the explanation were "Persia was backward," it could not also explain Byzantium. So the real cause has to be something shared, and something about the Arabs, not a Persian technology failure. That one fact dissolves most of the puzzle by itself.

So what were the real reasons?

Both empires had just wrecked each other. As we saw, the war of 602 to 628 left Persia and Byzantium exhausted, broke, and politically unstable. The Arabs arrived right afterward, against opponents at their weakest in centuries. Timing was not a detail; it was close to the whole thing.

Arabia had just been united, for the first time. Before Islam, the Arab tribes were famously fragmented, raiding and feuding among themselves. Islam welded them, inside a single generation, into one community under one command, and pointed their fighting energy outward instead of inward. The empires had brushed off Arab raiders for centuries. They had never faced a united Arabia. That was the genuinely new force on the field, and it was political and religious, not technological.

The Persians had fired their own desert experts. For generations the Sasanians had policed the desert frontier through an allied Arab buffer kingdom, the Lakhmids of al-Hira, who understood the tribes and guarded the empire's edge. Around 602, Khosrow II abolished that kingdom and took direct control. So at the exact moment a storm was gathering in Arabia, Persia had dismantled its own early-warning system and alienated the very Arabs who used to mind the gate. The warning came early: even before Islam, around 604 to 611, Arab tribes beat a Sasanian force at the Battle of Dhi Qar, a small but startling sign that the desert frontier was no longer safe. Some of those frontier Arabs would later ride with the Muslim armies.

The desert itself was a weapon. Heavy imperial forces could not chase a fast, camel-borne enemy into the deep desert. The Arabs could strike at the settled edge of Mesopotamia and then melt back into terrain where no slow, supply-hungry army could follow. They chose when and where to fight. That advantage owes nothing to equipment.

And the generals were good. This was not a mob carried only by zeal. Commanders like Khalid ibn al-Walid and Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas were genuinely skilled. Zeal gets soldiers to the battlefield; it does not win multi-day battles against armored cavalry. Skill does.

Now the battles themselves, with an honest warning attached. Almost everything we "know" about the day-by-day fighting at Qadisiyya comes from Arabic histories written more than two hundred years later, above all the great chronicler al-Tabari. They brim with vivid, instructive scenes: Persian war elephants crashing through the Arab lines until the Arabs learn to blind them and turn them on their own side; a sandstorm driving grit into Persian faces on the final day; the Persian general Rostam killed in the chaos. These tales may carry a kernel of truth, but they are also literature, shaped to teach and to thrill, and their numbers (this many tens of thousands a side) are almost certainly inflated and should not be trusted. So we can say with confidence that the Persians lost a decisive battle at Qadisiyya around 636 and that their capital, Ctesiphon, fell soon after. The movie-like details we hold at arm's length.

Was it a domino effect? Yes, in two stages. First the field battles: Qadisiyya broke the main army, and Nahavand around 642, which later writers called the "Victory of Victories," destroyed the last great force the empire could gather. After that there was simply no central army left to stop anyone. Then came a second, slower, less dramatic stage that matters just as much: province by province, city by city, the empire came apart, and a great deal of it fell without a battle at all. Local Persian lords and town leaders, the landed gentry called dehqans, often cut deals. They surrendered, agreed to pay tribute, and in return kept their lands, their local power, and usually their religion. To a regional noble, the empire in faraway Ctesiphon was already gone or worthless; making terms with the new power was simply the sensible move. The last king, Yazdegerd III, kept fleeing east trying to raise fresh armies and kept failing, partly because the provinces were quietly settling with the conquerors behind his back. He was finally killed near Merv in 651. The traditional story says a local miller murdered him for his jewels; other accounts say the miller was acting on the orders of the local governor, which would make it less a robbery than a quiet political execution. We do not know which is true. Either way it was no grand last stand: a tired, hunted man, killed for what he was worth to someone.

So the collapse was real, and it was fast, but it was not one clean sweep by a magically superior army. It was a broken superpower, caught at the worst moment in centuries, losing a few crucial battles to a newly united and unusually mobile enemy, and then unspooling through hundreds of local surrenders and bargains. No miracle. No secret weapon. And no need for one.

But all of that, the battles and the quiet surrenders alike, is still only clock number one.

Why did people actually convert? The slow clock

Here is where the popular story falls apart, and where the truth is genuinely more interesting.

If Iran was "conquered" and "became Muslim" in the 600s, you would expect the population to be mostly Muslim soon after, say within a generation or two. It was not. For a long time after the conquest, the overwhelming majority of people living in Iran were still not Muslim. They were still Zoroastrian, Christian, Jewish, and the rest. The Arab Muslims were a thin ruling layer on top of a vast non-Muslim population.

How do we even know this, since most ordinary people left no diary? This is the "how we know" question, and it is the best part. One scholar, Richard Bulliet, came up with a clever method. He studied biographical dictionaries, old reference books listing notable people, and tracked when families first started giving their sons distinctly Muslim names. A father named Ardashir (a Persian name) with a son named Muhammad is a small fossil of a conversion. Pile up enough of those name-shifts across the generations and you can sketch a rough curve of when conversion sped up.

What the curve suggests, and Bulliet was careful to call it an estimate, not a measurement, is that conversion to Islam in Iran was slow at first and only became widespread in the ninth and tenth centuries CE, two to three hundred years after the armies arrived. By Bulliet's reckoning, Iran may not have become majority Muslim until somewhere around the mid-900s. Other historians quibble with his exact numbers and his method, and they are right to, no single method should be trusted alone, but the big picture holds up against other evidence: the conquest was fast, the conversion was slow.

Why so slow? Because, in plain terms, mass forced conversion is mostly a myth, and the real reasons were quieter and more human:

It cost money to stay non-Muslim, but you were allowed to. Under Islamic rule, Jews, Christians, and (after some early argument) Zoroastrians were generally treated as "People of the Book," protected communities allowed to keep their religion in exchange for a special tax, the jizya, and a second-class legal status. This was not equality, and we should not romanticize it: it was a system designed to make non-Muslims pay for the privilege of staying non-Muslim, and to make Islam the path of least resistance. But it was also, crucially, not "convert or die." For the rulers, taxpaying non-Muslims were a revenue source; some governors actually disliked conversions because each new Muslim meant less tax money. People converted over generations partly to escape the tax, partly to get ahead in a society where being Muslim opened doors, partly through marriage, cities, and daily life, and yes, sometimes under social pressure and at moments of real persecution. Faith, money, and ambition were all braided together, the way they usually are in real human choices.

Conversion was a slow social process, not a switch. A family in a village does not change its deepest beliefs because a tax collector in a distant city now answers to a new ruler. It changes when the neighbors change, when the marriage market changes, when the language of getting ahead changes, over decades. That is why the curve bends upward two centuries late.

So: invasion, or something else?

Now we can answer the question the dinner table fights about, and the answer is both, but on different clocks, and you have to say which clock you mean.

The conquest of the state was an invasion. Foreign armies, real battles, an empire destroyed, a new ruling elite installed by force. Pretending otherwise, calling it a peaceful welcome, is nationalism of the rosy kind, erasing the violence.

The conversion of the people was not mainly an invasion or a forced mass baptism. It was a centuries-long, uneven, partly voluntary, partly pressured social transformation, driven as much by taxes, cities, marriage, and ambition as by anything done with a sword. Pretending that was a sudden violent imposition is nationalism of the bitter kind, turning a slow human process into a single act of foreign violence so it can be resented forever.

Both rosy and bitter stories are doing the same dishonest thing: collapsing two clocks into one so the past delivers a clean emotional verdict. The history refuses to deliver it. That refusal is not a disappointment. It is the lesson.

What we genuinely do not know

Be honest about the edges of the map, because they are real.

We do not know, with any precision, what most ordinary Iranians felt about any of this. Almost everyone who lived through the conquest and the long conversion left no record at all: the farmers, the women, the enslaved, the village priests. We catch them only sideways, in a tax list, a name on a page, a complaint in someone else's letter. Their inner lives are mostly lost to us, and anyone who tells you confidently "the Persian people felt X" about the conquest is filling silence with a guess.

We do not have a trustworthy account of the battles in close-up. Our detailed narratives of Qadisiyya and the rest were written down two centuries or more after the fact, by historians with their own purposes, and their troop numbers are almost surely invented. We know the outcomes far better than we know the days.

We argue about how much coercion there really was, and it varied enormously by time and place. There were certainly moments of destruction, persecution, and pressure, fire temples shut, communities squeezed, occasional revolts crushed. There were also long stretches and whole regions where non-Muslims lived for centuries with their faith intact. A single sentence cannot cover three centuries across a vast land, and we should distrust any sentence that claims to.

And we argue about Bulliet's numbers themselves, the exact shape and timing of that conversion curve. The pattern, fast conquest, slow conversion, is solid. The decimal points are not.

The open wound, and another way to carry it

Now the hardest part, the part this whole essay was really for.

For a lot of Iranians, what you just read does not feel like history. It feels like a wound that never closed: a proud, brilliant civilization broken by outsiders, its faith pushed aside, its very name almost lost. If that is how it feels in your family, the feeling is real and it deserves respect, not a lecture. But a feeling is not the same as a fact, and there are facts here that can change how the wound is carried. Not by denying it. By making it bigger and truer than the small story of "they beat us."

Start with the wound itself, and ask the question this essay keeps asking: who made this feeling, and when? The intense sense of the conquest as a national humiliation is, to a large degree, modern. It was sharpened in the 1800s and 1900s by Iranian nationalist thinkers, some of them borrowing European ideas about race and "Aryan" glory, who wanted a story of a pure golden age ruined by foreigners. Honesty cuts both ways here: resentment of the conquest is not only modern. There were medieval Iranians who chafed at Arab dominance (a whole literary movement, the Shu'ubiyya, argued for Persian dignity), and you can hear real grief in parts of the Shahnameh. But the picture of 651 as an open, bleeding, defining wound for an entire nation is mostly a recent construction, built by particular people for particular reasons. The wound is partly inherited and partly authored. Learning to tell which part is which is its own kind of freedom.

One famous version of the wound even has a title. In 1957 the Iranian historian Abdolhossein Zarrinkoub wrote a beautiful, mournful book called Two Centuries of Silence (Do Qarn-e Sokut), arguing that for two hundred years after the conquest the Persian voice went quiet under Arab rule. It is a powerful book, still widely read. But scholars pushed back hard, then and since, and Zarrinkoub himself softened the claim in a later edition. Their point: those centuries were not silent at all. They were roaring. Iranians were everywhere in the new civilization, simply writing in a new language for a while. Which brings us to the reframe that changes everything.

Iran lost a state. It did not lose itself, and it was not erased. Within a few generations, Iranians went from the conquered to the architects. This is the fact the open-wound story leaves out, and it is enormous:

  • When the first Arab dynasty, the Umayyads, was overthrown in 750, the revolution that did it rose in Khorasan, in eastern Iran, carried by largely Iranian discontent and led by a commander remembered as Abu Muslim al-Khorasani. The new rulers, the Abbasids, then ran their empire on a Persian model, with Persian-style administration and, for years, a brilliant Iranian family, the Barmakids, as their chief ministers. The "conquerors" were now being governed by the children of the "conquered."
  • Within about two centuries, native Iranian Muslim dynasties were ruling the plateau again: the Saffarids, the Samanids, the Buyids. Historians even have a name for this stretch, the "Iranian Intermezzo." The Buyids, a Persian dynasty, marched into Baghdad in 945 and kept the caliph, the supposed master of the whole Islamic world, as a powerless figurehead.
  • And the language came back, transformed. New Persian, the Persian still spoken today, was reborn in this period, now written in Arabic script and stocked with Arabic words, and it became one of the great world languages of culture. For a thousand years a "Persianate world" stretched from the Balkans through Central Asia to India, where emperors wrote poetry in Persian. Ferdowsi's Shahnameh (the "Book of Kings"), which deliberately carried the old pre-Islamic kings into this new world, is its crown jewel. And Nowruz, the Persian New Year rooted in the Zoroastrian past, is still celebrated by millions of Iranian Muslims every spring.

That is not the story of a people who were conquered and vanished. It is the story of a people who lost a government and then, astonishingly, captured a civilization from the inside, and reshaped it so deeply that we can no longer cleanly separate "Iranian" from "Islamic." The braid again: ruptured and continuous at the same time.

Now, do not let me sell you a rosy version either, because that is just nationalism wearing a different flag. There was real loss, and it should be named plainly. Zoroastrianism, the great faith of Sasanian Iran, shrank over the centuries into a small minority; temples and texts were lost; some Zoroastrians fled all the way to India, where their descendants, the Parsis, live to this day. Non-Muslims lived as second-class subjects who paid an extra tax. Things were destroyed that we will never get back, and people suffered who left no record of their suffering. Holding the gain without the loss is as dishonest as holding the loss without the gain. The grown-up view carries both in the same hand.

Then question the binary itself, one last time, because it is the sneakiest piece. "The Arabs defeated the Iranians" sounds clean, but the two sides were never that clean. Arabs had lived in Iran and Iraq long before Islam; Iranians joined the Muslim armies and the Muslim state early and eagerly; the people doing the fighting were mostly elites and soldiers, while most villagers' real loyalty was to their valley, not to any "nation." Over the centuries, Arab and Iranian and Turkic and a dozen other threads married and merged into what we later call Iran. There was no pure "us" waiting to be beaten by a pure "them." That purity is exactly the myth that makes the wound feel so simple.

So here is the other way to carry it, the one you can actually choose. The land we call Iran is one of the most conquered, most rebuilt places on Earth: Elamites, Medes, Persians, Greeks, Parthians, Sasanians, Arabs, Turks, Mongols, and on and on, each one supposedly "ending" Iran, and Iran absorbing each one and becoming something new. England does not bleed over the Norman conquest of 1066; it folded it in and moved forward. Treating 651 as a wound that must stay open forever is a choice, and not the only honest one. You can also see it as the most dramatic chapter in the oldest story this land knows how to tell: the story of being remade, again and again, and surviving because it kept being remade.

That second story is just as true as the wound. It has better evidence behind it. And it leaves a young person not with a grievance to inherit, but with something far stronger: the knowledge that you come from a place too deep and too stubborn to be ended by anyone, precisely because it has already survived being "ended" a dozen times.

One last twist, so the story never feels finished: the Iran of the conquest era was largely Sunni Muslim, not Shi'i. Iran's Shi'i majority is a much later creation, the work of a deliberate state project under the Safavid dynasty starting in 1501 CE, nearly nine hundred years after the Arab armies came. So even "Iran is a Shi'i country," which feels eternal, turns out to have a start date and a policy behind it. Almost nothing about a nation is as timeless as it feels. That, in the end, is the whole lesson.

The most respectful thing we can do with this history, then, is refuse to hand it over as a weapon, in any direction. The conquest of the state was real and violent, and we should not prettify it. The conversion of the people was real and slow and human and complicated. The loss was real, and so was the astonishing comeback. All of it is true at once. Holding all of it, instead of clutching one piece, is what it means to actually know this history, and to own it instead of being owned by it.

If this got you thinking, two companion questions are worth chasing next: how the Persian language clawed its way back through the Shahnameh (memory, not minutes), and the year 1501, when a teenage shah made Iran Shi'i on purpose.
