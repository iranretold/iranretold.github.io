The Mirror in the Sky

How people have pictured God, and why nobody can be sure they're right

Close your eyes and try to picture God. Go ahead, really try.

If you are like almost everyone who has ever lived, you pictured something that looks a lot like a person. Maybe an old man with a beard on a throne in the clouds. Maybe just a feeling of someone watching, someone who thinks and feels, who gets angry and shows mercy, the way people do. Almost nobody pictures a shapeless cloud of pure energy. Our minds simply do not work that way.

Hold onto that, because it is a clue and a warning at the same time. A clue, because the story of God turns out to be mostly the story of people looking up and seeing a reflection of themselves. A warning, because if we keep painting our own face onto the sky, we should be a little suspicious of how much we really know about what is up there.

This is a true story, as far as anyone can tell. But "as far as anyone can tell" is going to come up a lot. Some of the most important parts are things experts still argue about, or simply admit they cannot explain. That is not the boring part. That is the best part.

The first belief

Let's go way back, tens of thousands of years, to a man and a woman we can call Adam and Eve. Not the storybook ones, but real Stone Age humans. Their brains were exactly as big and as clever as yours. They hunted, they wove nets, they cooked over fires, and they buried their dead.

That last part matters more than it sounds. When someone died, these people did not just leave the body. They laid it out carefully, sprinkled it with red dust the color of blood, and placed favorite tools and necklaces beside it, as if the dead person would still need them somewhere else. Why bother, unless you believed the person was not really gone? That something invisible, something we might call a soul, had slipped out of the body and gone on living?

This belief, that you are more than just your body, is probably the oldest idea humans ever had. It is older than any god. And here is the honest part, the part most books skip: nobody knows where the idea came from. Not really. People have guessed for two hundred years. Maybe it came from dreams, where dead relatives still walk and talk. Maybe from something else. We don't know. Keep that question in your pocket, because the whole rest of the story is built on top of it.

The brain that makes gods

So how do you get from "I have a soul" to "there are gods in the trees"? Here is where your own brain quietly gives the game away.

Imagine you are walking through the woods at dawn and you see a face peering at you from the trees. Your heart slams. You freeze. Then you look again: it was just knots on a tree trunk. Phew.

Your brain is built to do that. It jumps to the idea that anything mysterious was caused by someone, some being with a mind. This was handy, because mistaking a tree for a tiger costs you nothing, while mistaking a tiger for a tree could get you eaten. Better safe than sorry.

Your brain does something else, too. The only mind you truly know from the inside is your own, so you use yourself as the model for everything else. If a thing has something like a face, you assume it has feelings and thoughts like you. That is why little kids talk to their toys. And it is why our ancestors, seeing a face in a tree, did not just see wood. They saw a spirit, with a soul like theirs.

One more trick: ideas spread best when they are mostly normal but a little bit strange in a useful way. A tree is boring. A tree that can talk and tell you the future? That is worth telling your friends about. So the slightly magical, very useful ideas survived and traveled, while the plain ones were forgotten.

Now for the surprise. For a long time, clever people assumed religion must exist because it helps us survive somehow: it calms our fears, or it glues a group together. But when scientists looked hard, those explanations kept falling apart. Religion is expensive. It costs time, food, and effort that could go into staying alive. So a growing number of researchers think religion was never a survival tool at all. It may be an accident: a side effect of a brain that was built for something else, for spotting danger and reading other minds. Those tricks kept us alive. Seeing gods may just be a leftover, like the way a machine built for one job sometimes does a second thing by mistake.

And notice what that does to our pocket question. If gods are a side effect of believing in souls, then why did we ever believe in souls in the first place? We are right back to the mystery, and it is still unsolved.

Building gods, and putting our own power into the sky

For a long time these spirits had no names. Then, about twelve thousand years ago, hunter-gatherers on a hill in what is now Turkey did something astonishing. They dragged enormous stones, some as heavy as ten tons, about the weight of two elephants, and stood them up in giant rings, carved with arms and belts. They are not pictures of people. They look like gods given a human shape. It is the oldest temple we have ever found.

Here is a twist worth handling with care. Building that place would have taken crowds of workers, and crowds need feeding. Some researchers think that feeding them is what first pushed humans to plant crops and herd animals. If they are right, it flips the usual story: belief in gods may have come before farming and helped invent it, not the other way around. Other researchers are not convinced. This is an argument, not a settled fact, and it is a good example of how shaky even the big "turning points" can be.

Once we pictured gods as people, we could not stop pouring more of ourselves into them. Gods got names, jobs, and families. They ate, argued, fell in love, and complained about work. And watch this pattern, because it repeats for thousands of years: the gods always seemed to be organized exactly like the humans who worshipped them. When people lived in small, free groups that argued things out together, the gods met in a kind of council and argued things out too. When kings took over on earth and ruled alone, a single king-god climbed to the top of heaven and all the other gods bowed down. Heaven kept rearranging its furniture to match the politics on the ground. People thought they were learning facts about the gods. Mostly, they were drawing a careful portrait of their own world.

The dangerous part

There is a catch in making God in your image. Whatever you put in comes back out looking holy.

Pour in your kindness, and you get a God of mercy. But pour in your fear, your hatred, your taste for revenge, and you get a God who wants your enemies crushed, and now you can tell yourself that wanting them crushed is holy too. This is why the very same religion, even the very same book, can turn one person gentle and another person cruel. The God is a mirror, and mirrors do not only reflect the nice things. That single idea explains a lot of human history, the beautiful parts and the bloody parts both.

The long, failed road to one God

Eventually a few people got uncomfortable with gods who acted so human. A Greek thinker named Xenophanes joked that if horses could draw, they would draw their gods as horses. He had spotted the secret: we were just making gods in our own image. He and others wanted a single God with no body and no moods, a pure force behind everything.

Here is something that surprises most people: for almost the entire human story, hardly anyone believed in one single God. The idea is young, only about three thousand years old, a newcomer. And the first times anyone seriously tried it, it flopped.

An Egyptian pharaoh named Akhenaten declared there was only one god, the sun disk, and tried to erase all the others by force. The moment he died, his people threw the idea out and scrabbled to forget him. Around the same era, an Iranian prophet named Zarathustra (you may know him as Zartosht) had a vision of one wise Lord standing above all the old gods. His idea slowly twisted into a belief in two gods instead, one good and one evil. Both attempts failed for the same simple reason: a God with no face, no feelings, and no needs is almost impossible for ordinary people to love. Our brains crave a God we can talk to.

So how did belief in one God ever survive? Not by winning an argument. By surviving a disaster. A small people called Israel were conquered, their temple burned, their nation wrecked. By the rules of the time, that meant their God had lost and should be dumped for the winner's stronger god. Instead, a few stubborn believers flipped the whole story: there had never been any other gods to lose to. Their God was the only God, maker of light and darkness, good and bad, all at once. The first lasting belief in one God was not a triumph. It was a refusal to give up, born in the ashes of defeat. It easily could have gone the other way, and almost did.

(One side note, exactly the kind of thing this story keeps bumping into: a famous part of this people's history is a tale of slaves escaping Egypt, led by a man named Moses. Yet diggers have never found a single trace of those slaves in Egypt, in a kingdom that wrote down nearly everything. Maybe it happened differently. Maybe a memory grew into a story over centuries. We honestly argue about it.)

From there the story splits. One faith took the boldest step of all and said the one and only God had become an actual human being, who got hungry, walked the earth, and died. Another faith swept out of the Arabian desert insisting, fiercely, that God is One, with no equal and nothing like Him, yet even there people could not stop describing God as having a face and hands. The pull to make God human is just that strong.

And inside that last faith, some mystics, including the Persian poet Rumi, asked a daring question. If God is truly One, and God made everything, then where exactly does God stop and the universe begin? Their answer turned everything inside out: maybe there is no line. Maybe God did not make the universe from the outside. Maybe God is the universe, and everything in it, including you.

What we honestly do not know

Before you decide what to do with all this, look straight at the holes, because they are real and they are big.

We do not know where the idea of a soul came from, and that is the seed the whole thing grew from. We do not really know what those cave paintings meant; the famous antlered figure some call "the first picture of God" is one scholar's hunch, not a proven fact. We do not know for sure whether that ancient temple invented farming or just happened to stand nearby. We do not know if the escaped-slaves story is history or memory or myth.

And the biggest hole of all: nobody has ever proven that God exists, and nobody has ever proven that God does not. Anyone who tells you they have settled that question, in either direction, is selling something.

So, make your choice

Here is where the long story leaves you, and it leaves you with a fork, not an answer.

Follow the "God is all" idea to its end and you reach one view: there is no line between you and the universe and God, and everything that exists is part of a single living whole. Look at the very same facts another way and you reach the opposite view: it is all a magnificent accident of atoms, with no one steering, beautiful precisely because it just happened. Here is the honest part. Both of those are guesses. Both fit the evidence we have. Neither can be proven, not today, maybe not ever.

Think of light passing through a glass prism, splitting into red, orange, blue, and green. The colors look completely different, but they are all the same light from the same source. That image is how the "all is one" people see the world. It is gorgeous. It is also just a way of seeing, not a proof.

So the choice is yours, and it is a real one. Not what is fashionable, not what someone scares you into, but an actual choice you get to make once you understand how strange, how human, and how uncertain this whole long story really is. When you look up and see something like yourself looking back, it might be a clue about what is really there. Or it might be a trick of the mirror. Figuring out which, for yourself, is the oldest homework there is.

Five questions worth arguing about

  1. Our brains are built to "see" minds and faces where there are none. Does that mean God must be a mistake? Or could a brain that is good at noticing minds sometimes notice a real one? How would you even tell the difference?

  2. For thousands of years, people imagined gods that were organized just like their own kings and councils. If you drew a picture of God based only on the world you live in right now, what would it look like, and what would that say about us?

  3. The same holy book can make one reader kinder and another reader crueler. Is that the book's fault, the reader's fault, or nobody's fault? Can an idea be blamed for what people do with it?

  4. For most of human history almost no one believed in a single God, and the first people who tried the idea failed. Does an idea being old, or popular, or finally winning, make it any more likely to be true?

  5. Two explanations, "everything is one living whole" and "everything is a beautiful accident," both fit the evidence and neither can be proven. If you cannot prove it either way, how should a person decide what to believe? And once they decide, should they hold that belief tightly, or loosely?

The ideas in this essay grew out of my own reading of Reza Aslan's God: A Human History.
