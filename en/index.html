Iran Retold

Critical essays for young readers. Each piece looks honestly at what we know, how we know it, and where the evidence runs out and the guessing begins. Read them in any order. Every essay also exists in Persian.

The Mirror in the Sky

A history of how human beings have pictured God, starting from the oldest idea of all, the soul, moving through brains built to see minds everywhere, to the long and mostly failed road to a single God. People keep painting their own face onto the sky.

Main questions: Why do we picture God as a person, and what does that tell us about God versus about us? If nobody can prove it either way, how should a person decide what to believe?

The Land That Kept Changing Its Gods

Iran changed its gods again and again, the old Iranian deities, Zoroaster's Ahura Mazda, Judaism, Christianity, Mani's light, the slow arrival of Islam, the Safavid turn to Shi'ism, the Baha'i Faith, and yet never quite threw the old ones away. Religion here is a braid, not a staircase.

Main questions: Why did one piece of land change its gods so many times, and what survived each change? How much of "what ancient Iranians believed" can we really know, when only kings and priests left records?

Zoroaster: The Prophet Who Slips Through Our Fingers

One of history's most important prophets is also a man we can barely date, can't quite locate, and may not even be the author of his own hymns. The essay keeps the questioning poet of the Gathas separate from the later miracle-baby legend, and weighs the thrilling claim that he invented monotheism, heaven, and hell.

Main questions: How can someone be this important and this unknowable? Was he really "the first monotheist," and did he shape Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, or is that a story we tell for our own reasons?

The Achaemenids: An Empire of Many Peoples

The first great Persian empire, from Cyrus's conquests to Alexander's blow, told not as a parade of kings but as a working machine: roads, satrapies, scribes, seals, rations, and dozens of peoples held together by accommodation more than by force. It surfaces the ordinary operators of empire (Arshama, Parnakka, Irdabama, Mibtahiah, the soldiers at Elephantine) and closes with a one-page summary, a detailed timeline, and a glossary.

Main questions: How did one Iranian family hold so many languages, gods, and lands together without forcing them all to be the same? How do we actually know how the empire ran, from clay tablets and letters rather than royal boasts? Was its fall sudden, or long in the making?

How Iran Became a Muslim Country

Two clocks, not one: the Persian state fell in about fifteen years, but Iranians took three to four centuries to become Muslim. The essay separates conquest from conversion, dissolves the "secret weapon" puzzle of how lightly armed Arabs beat a superpower, and offers a way to carry the wound without inheriting a grievance.

Main questions: Was it invasion or embrace? How could the Arabs possibly win? Why did conversion take so long, and who decided that 651 should feel like an open wound?

Two Kings and a Stolen Throne: The Pahlavi Era

For fifty-four years, two kings tried to build a modern Iran by reaching back over a thousand years of Islam to grab the glory of the ancient Persians. They built railways, schools, and an army; they also jailed their critics and, in the end, lost everything. The essay turns on 1953, the most argued-about year in modern Iranian history.

Main questions: Was Reza Shah a builder or a bully, or one man who was both? How much of the 1953 coup was foreign and how much was Iranian? Was Iran really an unbroken 2,500-year monarchy?
